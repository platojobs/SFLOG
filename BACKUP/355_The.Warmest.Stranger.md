# [The Warmest Stranger](https://github.com/platojobs/SFLOG/issues/355)

**The Warmest Stranger**

In the eyes of patients, what role does a doctor actually play—a warm angel or a cold machine? For a long time after I started working, I was unable to find a good answer, until one patient appeared.

This patient was a sculpture artist teaching at a university; let us call him Teacher Zhang. Teacher Zhang unfortunately contracted lung cancer, and our director performed surgery on him. In the first two years after the operation, he recovered very well and was able to paint and make sculptures again. He was extremely happy; in his words, art was his life. This good state lasted two years. Two years later, Teacher Zhang's condition changed: fluid appeared in his chest cavity, and the situation was not optimistic. Teacher Zhang needed to take a drug to control the progression of the disease, and this drug cost 10,000 yuan a month. But after taking it for a month, Teacher Zhang said he did not want to continue the medication, because the drug had a side effect: it made him break out in a rash that was very itchy. Teacher Zhang was troubled by the skin itching, which seriously affected his creative inspiration and creative passion. So he insisted on stopping the medication.

A cancer patient refusing treatment in order to be able to paint and make sculptures—I was shocked. Without taking the drug, his condition would certainly be uncontrollable. After repeated persuasion, Teacher Zhang still refused to take the medication. And so another three years passed, and Teacher Zhang's condition clearly deteriorated. He developed pleural effusion, abdominal effusion, and bone metastasis of cancer cells—all manifestations of advanced tumors. I again suggested that he take the medication. By this time Teacher Zhang could no longer paint or make sculptures, so that was no longer his reason for not taking the drug. But Teacher Zhang still refused to take the medication. He admitted frankly that, apart from worrying about the side effects of the drug, he was also worried about the cost.

A few more months passed, and Teacher Zhang, accompanied by his wife, came to my consulting room. Teacher Zhang was already unable to move conveniently; he needed help both to sit down and to get up. At this time I again emphasized the urgency of taking the medication and frankly told him that the danger of death was already approaching. Teacher Zhang firmly rejected my proposal. He said: "I know there is no possibility of a cure for me. If I exhaust the family's savings on medication, it would at most briefly prolong my life—but what then? How would my wife live afterward? I am not afraid of death. I came today only to ask: will my illness lead to long-term bed confinement, requiring my wife to take care of my daily life?" I truthfully told Teacher Zhang that in the end the condition would deteriorate very quickly and that the situation he worried about would not occur. In that moment, Teacher Zhang was not nervous at all; instead, he was relieved. After receiving my answer, Teacher Zhang with difficulty rose, took his leave of me, and solemnly extended his hand to shake mine. I also solemnly responded. Perhaps we both had a premonition that this would be the last time we met. After that, I never saw Teacher Zhang again. Two months later, I learned the news of Teacher Zhang's death.

Teacher Zhang was a patient I encountered who clearly knew his own goals in life. From the beginning, when he refused to take medication with side effects for the sake of his artistic creation, to later, when he refused expensive medication so that his family could live better, he made choices. It is hard to judge whether Teacher Zhang's choices were right or wrong, but one thing is certain: when he made his choices, he had fully grasped the relevant information about the disease, the side effects and costs of treatment, and the potential consequences of giving up treatment. Later, when I looked through books, I found that this model—in which doctors provide professional information and doctors and patients jointly discuss treatment plans—is called the shared decision-making model. The characteristic of this model is that doctors focus not only on the disease itself but also on the patient's feelings. Doctors tend to care about how patients understand the current condition and its potential consequences, what fears and hopes patients have, and what their most important goals are under the current circumstances. I am very glad that, without understanding these theories, I had already provided this kind of medical service to Teacher Zhang. I am even more glad that, as a doctor, I did not arbitrarily negate the patient's choices.

In the outpatient clinic I met a well-dressed, courteous middle-aged man who came to consult about his elderly father's condition. His father had advanced lung cancer. Looking through the medical records, I found that many doctors had already guided the old man's treatment plan. But this middle-aged man still kept registering and kept consulting about his father's condition. In our conversation I found that he already knew the pros and cons of various treatment plans like the back of his hand, so his questions were not about obtaining information on the disease and treatment. So why did he repeatedly consult different doctors?

Through further understanding, I learned that his father had raised him through great hardship. Now he had achieved success in his career, and his happy life had just begun when he learned the terrible news that his father had lung cancer. Deep in his heart, he felt that he had been busy with his career and had neglected his father's health, so he kept consulting, wanting to learn about more advanced treatment plans to make up for his own negligence. I patted him on the shoulder and said: "This is not your fault. Lung cancer has the characteristic of hidden onset; at least 80% of patients are already at an advanced stage when discovered. You are a filial son. You have already done your best." Hearing my words, this middle-aged man actually burst into loud weeping. I feel that, as a doctor, the most meaningful experience comes from helping others deal with problems that medicine cannot solve.

However, things are not always so simple. For advanced malignant tumors, we often fall into a helpless predicament. I will never forget the expectant look in the eyes of patients' relatives when they ask, "Doctor, is there any other treatment?" Nor will I forget how, upon learning "there is currently no way," that shining gaze immediately turns to astonishment and then slowly dims. For quite a long time after I began independently seeing patients in the outpatient clinic, I was very afraid of that gaze; it seemed to be reproaching the doctor for being incompetent. But gradually, I adjusted my mindset. I know that telling patients and their relatives the true condition is an important responsibility of mine as a doctor. Only by providing this true information can we avoid the harm that excessive treatment brings to patients, and even enable a family to grasp the last precious time together.

As doctors, we have always felt that our work is to guard health and preserve life. In fact, we should have a farther-reaching goal, and that goal is to help people be happy. Many patients meet me only once, and often our time together is only a few minutes. In those brief few minutes, they are willing to reveal their most vulnerable side to a stranger. I believe this is because of trust, and also because of hope. I am willing to be that warmest stranger.
