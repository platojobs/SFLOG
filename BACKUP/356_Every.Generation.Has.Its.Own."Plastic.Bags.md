# [Every Generation Has Its Own "Plastic Bags](https://github.com/platojobs/SFLOG/issues/356)

**Every Generation Has Its Own "Plastic Bags"**

My professor said that she used to be unable to stand her mother's habit of hoarding plastic bags. Every plastic bag, big and small, was tucked away by her mother wherever there was space, and not a single one was thrown out. One day she lifted her mother's bed and saw that the space under the bed board was covered with all kinds of plastic bags. In that moment she blew up, and the long-accumulated confusion and complaints finally erupted. She threw away all the plastic bags her mother had saved in one go, and the two of them quarreled and gave each other the cold shoulder because of it.

Then one day, her child said to her: "When are you going to clear some of the books off your bookshelf?"

In that moment she understood: her child's protest against her was just like her own protest against her mother.

Was what the child could not stand really the plastic bags under the bed, or the messy books on the bookshelf? I think it was neither. What the child could not stand was the mother.

So no matter what a mother likes to collect, her child may feel it is unnecessary, that it takes up space, that it is boring. Those things can only show your ignorance and backwardness, carrying the stamp of an old-fashioned, faded era.

I thought about it: do I have any "plastic bags"? Actually, yes—I like buying cups. Every time I go to a museum or an art gallery, when leaving I always want to bring back a little souvenir. After browsing the gift shop, I will most likely pick out a cup.

I could not help thinking: if one day, when I pick up another cup in some museum gift shop, my son suddenly says, "Can you buy a few fewer cups?" and then my husband adds, "How many cups are there at home already!"—just thinking about it, I feel I should start doing psychological preparation now, so that when the time comes I can throw out a whole set of psychological-need logic to prove the rationality of my cup buying.

In my eyes, buying cups is reasonable. But what psychology lies behind hoarding plastic bags?

I pondered this question for a long time, and then decided not to think about it anymore. Because I realized that if I cannot understand the older generation's habit of hoarding plastic bags, that means that no matter how reasonable my explanation is, my son will not be able to understand my habit of collecting cups.

No generation can fully empathize with another generation—this is the essence of the matter.

At the supermarket near my home, basically every day early in the morning there are people waiting for it to open, mostly old men and old women going in to buy discounted eggs. That day I said to my husband: "If one day I become this unreasonable, you must stop me."

Then one day, we passed a long line of people waiting to buy LABUBU plush dolls, and I wanted to line up and buy one too. My husband said: "You might as well line up at the supermarket entrance in the morning." You see, every generation has its own "eggs" to scramble for.

We simply instinctively look down on the previous generation's values. Anything they take delight in, we naturally reject—just as we learn to discriminate against our parents' behavior of hoarding plastic bags without any education at all.

A friend of mine likes to hoard tutoring materials. From her child's elementary school through middle school, the after-school tutoring materials she hoarded have filled every corner of the house. Now her daughter has just been admitted to high school, and one day she suddenly asked her: "When are you going to sell all those useless tutoring materials?"
